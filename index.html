“Fall Asleep To”

A data report on the sleep-content niche — whether a two-person, talking-over-Minecraft channel can work, what the outliers actually are, and exactly what to upload first.

The verdict in 60 seconds

Yes — the concept works, and there is hard proof. The exact format (“Fall asleep while I play [game]” + a calm voice talking over it) is a real, proven, currently-growing niche. Across 75 videos using that exact framing, the median video has 240,893 views and 71% clear 100,000 views. A 20,100-subscriber channel put up one and got 607,561 views — 30× its own subscriber count.

The single most valuable number in this report is 5.6×. One creator (MadMorph, 851k subs) posts both sleep-framed and normal videos on the same channel, same games, same voice. His sleep-framed videos have a median of 235,000 views. His normal videos: 42,000. That is a controlled experiment — the framing alone is worth 5.6×.

The one real risk is that you are two people. Across ~225 results, there is no successful purpose-built two-person sleep channel. That is either your opening or your warning, and this report explains which — plus the one production fix that decides it.

01 · Method

What was actually measured

Nobody can scan “the entirety of YouTube” — there is no public API that exposes it. What was done instead is a broad, real-data sweep: over 100 search queries run directly against YouTube, every result’s live view count and duration pulled, then 22 full channel back-catalogues dumped video-by-video (up to 200 videos each). Every number in this report is a real, pulled number. Nothing is estimated or remembered.

4,000+videos with live view counts and durations
557spoken-word sleep videos after filtering out rain / white noise / music
75videos using the exact “fall asleep while I…” format
22channel back-catalogues dumped in full
Read this before you trust any number YouTube search ranks by relevance and popularity, so a search-built sample is biased upward — it over-represents winners. That is corrected for in two ways below: by comparing videos within a single channel (a controlled test), and by reporting the failure rate of the format, not just its stars. Where a figure could not be verified, it is labelled Unverified rather than smoothed over.

His own uploads cluster at 143 minutes median, and 2–3 hours is his best bucket on both median and p90. He has effectively already run this experiment for you.

Across the 75 exact-format videos, the shortest is 57 minutes, the median is 135 minutes, the longest 346 minutes. Nothing under an hour appears in this format at all. The dead zone is 20–40 minutes — median 65,766 views across 87 videos, the worst bucket in the entire dataset.

Why not 8 hours, when 8h+ has the highest median of all? A wider sweep of 1,065 genre videos found the 8h+ bucket really does have the top median (828,441). But it is owned almost entirely by five legacy ambient channels — Relaxing White Noise, Calm, Soothing Relaxation, Yellow Brick Cinema — with a decade of search authority on “10 hours of rain” queries. Not one spoken-word channel in the entire corpus reaches that bucket. Meanwhile every talking channel measured — Sleepy History, Jason Stephenson, Critikal Chronicles, MadMorph — locks onto 2–3 hours and stays there across its whole upload history regardless of era. That convergence is your answer. Competing at 8 hours means a format war against SEO moats you cannot out-rank; 2–3 hours is where voices actually win.

One more useful null result: across four fully-dumped channels, the correlation between a video’s length and its views within a channel is effectively zero (Pearson r between −0.14 and +0.18). Once you are in the right band, going longer is not a lever. Stop optimising length after two hours and put the effort into the hook instead.

The number to build around Target 2 hours to 2 hours 30 minutes. That is one recording session, no editing beyond topping and tailing, and it sits precisely on the best-performing bucket in both datasets. Do not pad to 8 hours — it does not pay, and it hurts the ratio of talk to dead air.

05 · The two-person problem

Your biggest risk, and the fix

This is the part to read twice, because it is the one thing about your specific plan that the data pushes back on.

Finding Across roughly 225 results from nine targeted searches for two-person sleep content, not one genuine, purpose-built, from-scratch two-friend sleep channel was found. Every multi-person result at scale is repurposed famous audio — Karl Pilkington and Ricky Gervais re-cut as sleep content, Would I Lie To You? compilations, a Joe Pera loop. Those work because the voices are already familiar, which is exactly the thing a new channel cannot borrow. All seven of the biggest “talking over Minecraft footage” channels are solo.

Why it is empty — and it is not just that nobody tried

The mechanism that makes background audio work for sleep is a steady, low-variance noise floor. Sleep researchers and the creators who have optimised for it converge on the same point: the audio’s job is to occupy a racing mind, not to entertain it. The named failure mode in listener complaints is sudden loudness — a laugh spike, an interruption, a shout. Two friends bantering is structurally more prone to exactly that than one person narrating.

But the counter-evidence is real, and it is in your favour
  • MadMorph × Down To Sleep — a two-person collaboration inside this exact format — returned a median of 407,306 views over two videos, higher than MadMorph’s solo median. Two voices are not disqualifying.
  • sleepyslorgs proves the funny half: “Fall Asleep While I Sell More Drugs” (447,467), “…Rob Rich People” (556,189), “…Operate Heavy Artillery” (598,004). Median 341,108 on 154k subs. Comedy sells in this niche.
  • Comedic personal-story content is enormous adjacent to it: Tyler Vitelli’s “Embarrassing School Moments We All Had” has 5.4M views, and his “1 hour of Tyler’s best stories to eat & sleep to” has 3.35M. The Kalogeras Sisters’ two-person embarrassing-story video has 3.8M.
The warning shot: a famous sleep podcast gets 582 views a video on YouTube Sleep With Me is the best-known sleep podcast there is — deliberately monotone, built to be boring on purpose. On YouTube it has 40,500 subscribers and a median of 582 views across 200 videos, with a best of 1,500. That is not a failure of the content; it is proof that uploading a podcast to YouTube is not the same product as making a YouTube sleep video. The channels winning here are built around watchable calm footage with a voice on top. The gameplay is not decoration — it is the reason the video works on this platform. Do not think of this as “our podcast, with Minecraft behind it.”
The single production decision that resolves this The gap in the market is not “two friends talking” — that is a podcast. It is two friends talking with the dynamics of a sleep aid. Concretely, and this is the whole technical brief:
  • Hard-limit and compress the mix so laughs cannot spike. Target roughly −16 LUFS integrated with a true peak ceiling around −1.5 dBTP and a tight loudness range. A laugh should be audible, never louder.
  • Never both raise your voice at once. No shouting, no “NO WAY”, no talking over each other.
  • Game audio very low or off. No sudden creeper, no damage sounds, no death screens.
  • Record on separate tracks so you can level each other independently afterwards.
Get that right and you are the first proper entrant in a format the data says is empty. Get it wrong and you are a podcast with a misleading title, which is the 80-views outcome.

06 · Content & structure

What they actually say, and in what order

Real auto-captions were pulled from seven top videos in the genre and the opening 60–90 seconds read line by line. The conventions are unusually consistent.

Convention 1 — get to content in one sentence

There is no “hey guys, welcome back” anywhere in the sample. Six of the seven skip greeting entirely and open cold on the first fact or the first line of the story.

“this is your warning to never be friends with a bilingual person because I have a friend that speaks English and Spanish…” Tyler Vitelli — comedic storytime, 3.35M views. First words of the video.
“welcome to another riveting story time from my compendium of social ineptitude — today’s story takes place during high school when I thought break dancing was the dopest…” Critikal Chronicles — 3.4M views.

Convention 2 — the sleep promise lives in the title, not the script

Only one of the seven said anything resembling a sleep ritual out loud. That one is the market leader, and it is worth copying:

“in this video I’m going to play Minecraft and talk to you gently while you fall asleep… this is episode 5 of my fall asleep with series… let’s tuck you in — take a nice deep breath for me — and let’s get down to sleep with Minecraft.” MadMorph — 3.4M views. The full opening: format explainer, series number, ritual cue, ~20 seconds.

Convention 3 — the gameplay gets referenced, the wallpaper does not

This splits cleanly. Videos with live gameplay behind them talk to it constantly — it is what stops the talk track feeling like a podcast:

“back up a tree again as always… I’ve turned the render distance up — what is that, is that a horse? I see a horse in the distance, let’s swim over and say hello.” MadMorph, mid-video.

Videos with static images or stock footage never mention the visuals at all. Since you are playing Minecraft live, you are in the first camp — react to the game between story beats. It is free filler, it is what makes the format not-a-podcast, and it gives you somewhere to go when a story runs out.

Convention 4 — nothing must depend on having heard the last ten minutes

Every performing format here is structurally dip-in-and-out-of: facts in a list, Reddit threads back to back, unrelated anecdotes stacked. No cliffhangers, no running plot, no “remember when I said…”. The viewer is going to lose consciousness mid-sentence and must lose nothing by it. Your “worst rizz attempts” idea is perfectly shaped for this — it is a stack of independent anecdotes. Your “graduation went wrong” idea is a single narrative, which is weaker for the format unless you pad it out with surrounding school stories.

Convention 5 — “No Ads” is a selling point

Multiple high performers put (No Ads) or No Adverts directly in the title. Creators only advertise that when the opposite is a known complaint — being woken up by a mid-roll. Keep this in your back pocket: it is a real differentiator once you are monetised and can choose.

07 · Titles

Your two ideas, graded — and 15 to start with

“Fall asleep to how my high school graduation went wrong”

Rework it

Right instinct, wrong shape. Problems: it is a single story, so it cannot sustain two hours; “went wrong” is vague, and the format rewards a specific promise. Also the winning verb pattern is “while I / while we”, not “to how”.

Better: “Fall asleep while we tell you every school story we’ve never told anyone” — the graduation disaster becomes the opening anecdote and the thumbnail hook, not the whole video.

“Fall asleep to our worst rizz attempts”

Strong — lead with this

This is the better of the two and it should be video one. It is a stack of independent anecdotes (perfect for the format), it is inherently two-person, it is funny in the low-stakes embarrassing register that demonstrably works, and “rizz” carries its own search demand.

Tightened: “Fall asleep while we tell you our worst rizz attempts (2 hours)”

The title formula, from the data

Every high performer in the exact-format set follows one pattern: “Fall Asleep While I/We [verb + specific thing]”, optionally with a length or parenthetical. Long titles are fine — the winners run 40–70 characters. The verb is doing the work: “Sell More Drugs”, “Rob Rich People”, “Operate Heavy Artillery”, “Build a Nuclear Bomb in My Garden”. Specific and slightly absurd beats calm and generic.

07b · Thumbnails

The visual system, measured

The top 15 thumbnails in the niche were downloaded and looked at directly. The convention is far more consistent than in most niches — and it is the opposite of standard YouTube advice.

86%dark / low-key palette
71%specifically blue, teal or navy
7%contain a real human face
36%use moon / stars / night sky

Only one of fifteen had a real face on it. Counting cartoon and animal faces, still only three. 71% carry text, but never more than two to four words — bold sans-serif, white on dark, never a sentence. The dominant formula is: no face, dark blue palette, minimal bold text, one night motif. A calm mood shot, not a shocked-face thumbnail.

Your thumbnail spec
  • Base: a still from your own Minecraft footage, colour-graded dark — desaturate and push towards navy/near-black. Night scenes, lanterns, rain, a lit window.
  • No face close-ups. If you want to signal “two people”, use two silhouettes or two Minecraft characters sitting down — not smiling photos of you both.
  • Text: two short lines max. The hook word and the twist word. Large bold sans-serif, white, thin dark outline. Put the runtime here as a small tag.
  • One accent colour only, and one small moon-or-stars cue. That is the entire night signal.
  • No red arrows, no circles, no oversaturation. In this niche, restraint is the trust signal — a loud thumbnail reads as “this will keep me awake”.

08 · Money

The monetisation reality

Verified against YouTube’s own policy documentation. The good news is that the format is safe; the caution is that the money per view is not the attraction — the watch hours are.

Policy: you are clear Verified On 15 July 2025 YouTube renamed its “repetitious content” rule to “inauthentic content”. It targets templated, AI-slideshow, near-zero-effort mass production. Original commentary over gameplay is explicitly fine under the separate, unchanged reused content policy, which permits monetisation where there is “significant original commentary, editing, or educational/entertainment value”. Two friends telling their own stories over their own Minecraft footage is about as original as it gets. No YouTube policy document names sleep content at all — it is judged per video, not by genre.
Minecraft audio: officially fine, practically imperfect Mojang’s usage guidelines permit monetised use of official Minecraft music in game-related content. But real Content ID false claims have hit named creators on C418 tracks. Over a 2-hour video that is a lot of exposure. Turn the in-game music off and use royalty-free ambience — you lose nothing and remove the risk entirely.
Time-sensitive — the bar is about to double Act before Feb 2027 The YouTube Partner Programme currently needs 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 watch hours in 12 months, or 10 million Shorts views in 90 days (note: 10 million, not the 3 million figure that circulates). From 1 February 2027 those thresholds double to 8,000 watch hours and 20 million Shorts views. If you start now and hit 4,000 hours before then you are grandfathered in under the easier bar; if you drift, you need twice as much. This is the single strongest argument for starting immediately rather than in the new year.

The watch-hours arithmetic — this is the actual argument for long videos

Long sleep videos are extraordinarily efficient at clearing the watch-hour bar, because the viewer is asleep and the tab keeps running.

Assume sleep RPM is below gaming and commentary — the audience is asleep, which is bad for advertisers — and treat ad revenue as a bonus rather than the plan. YouTube’s Sleep Timer works against you slightly Verified The Sleep Timer is real and now available to everyone, not just Premium. It auto-pauses playback — which plausibly cuts off later mid-roll opportunities in exactly the long videos this genre relies on. Not a blocker, but another reason not to model this as an ad-revenue business early on.

09 · Shorts

What to clip, and why Shorts matter here

Shorts are not where this niche lives — long-form is. But they are the cheapest discovery you have, and your format generates them for free.

The structural insight Nobody falls asleep to a Short. So do not make sleep Shorts — make funny Shorts that advertise a sleep video. Your two-hour recording contains 15–20 clippable punchlines. The Short sells the joke; the pinned comment and the end screen sell the two-hour version. You are using the comedy lane for discovery and the sleep lane for retention, which is exactly the arbitrage the 5.6× number in §02 describes.

Five formats to cut from every recording

  1. The single worst attempt. 30–45 s, one complete anecdote with a punchline. Text hook on frame one: “the worst thing I have ever said to a girl”. Minecraft footage behind it.
  2. The reaction clip. Your friend’s genuine response to your story. Two-person content outperforms solo in Shorts even though it underperforms in sleep long-form — different rules, use both.
  3. The “we shouldn’t have said that” clip. Cut on the moment one of you regrets speaking. High rewatch.
  4. The gameplay-fail sync. A punchline that lands exactly as something goes wrong in Minecraft. Free comic timing — watch for these while recording and note the timestamp.
  5. The direct trailer. 20 s, three rapid story titles, ending on “…all two hours are on the channel.” Post this one last, once the long video is live.
The one thing to get right mechanically Use YouTube’s “Related video” link on every Short so it points at the long-form upload — it is the only native path YouTube documents, it is explicitly scoped to videos on your own channel, and most people never set it. Back it up with a pinned comment and a spoken call-out. Do not split Shorts onto a second channel — you would forfeit that link for a “cleaner algorithm signal” that nothing in the research substantiates.
Honest limits on the Shorts data Weakest section Shorts are the least well-evidenced part of this report and it would be dishonest to dress it up. Of 752 shorts-targeted results, only 102 were genuinely short-form with view counts, the overall median was 50 views, and the p90 was 2.3 million — an extreme power law where almost everything dies. Worse, the specific combination you are planning (storytime narration over Minecraft, cut to a Short) returned only 12 matching titles and they were nearly all dead, median 6 views. That is not evidence it fails — keyword search is a bad instrument for finding that sub-format — but it is not evidence it works either. Where the buckets had real sample size, 90–180 second Shorts beat 60–90 second ones on both median and p90, so lean long within Shorts. Treat Shorts as cheap optionality, not as the growth plan.

10 · The plan

First 90 days

Before you
record
  • Set up separate audio tracks for each of you. This is the one thing that cannot be fixed later.
  • Turn in-game music off. Lower game SFX until they sit clearly under your voices.
  • Pick a calm Minecraft activity that lasts hours and never spikes: building, terraforming, a peaceful-mode world. Not PvP, not combat, not anything with a death sound.
  • Write a list of 25–30 story prompts. You will burn through more than you think in two hours.
Weeks 1–4
Prove the format
1 long video per week. 2–2.5 hours each. 3–4 Shorts per week cut from it.
  • Week 1: Fall asleep while we tell you our worst rizz attempts
  • Week 2: Fall asleep while we tell you every school story we’ve never told anyone (graduation story opens it)
  • Week 3: Fall asleep while we play Minecraft and rank our worst dates
  • Week 4: Fall asleep while we build a house and confess everything
Do not judge anything yet. Sleep content is search-driven and back-loads views over months.
Weeks 5–8
Find the hook
Same cadence. Now look at which of the four titles pulled best and make two more of that one. Watch the retention graph for the first 60 seconds specifically — that is where sleep videos are won or lost. Add a chapter list. Start putting the runtime in the thumbnail.
Weeks 9–12
Compound
Build a playlist that autoplays through the night — this is the highest-leverage thing in the niche and almost nobody does it properly. Aim for 12–14 long videos live by day 90. Around here the watch-hour threshold should fall out naturally from the arithmetic in §08.

The five ways this fails

  1. Volume spikes. One genuine laugh at full level wakes the viewer and they never come back. This is the number-one risk and it is entirely a mixing problem.
  2. Too short. Anything under an hour does not exist in this format. A 40-minute upload is a podcast with the wrong title.
  3. Quitting at week 6. Sleep content accrues views over months through search, not in the first 48 hours. Judging it on day-one performance is the standard way people abandon a working channel.
  4. Being interesting. Genuinely — cliffhangers and running plots are a bug here. Nothing may depend on staying awake.
  5. Copying MadMorph’s tone instead of his framing. His gentle solo register is his moat. Yours is being two friends who are funny at low volume. Take the title structure and the runtime; do not take the whisper.
Bottom line The concept is validated by a controlled, within-channel 5.6× and a 240,893-view median across the format. A 20,100-sub channel pulled 607,561 views on one upload.
